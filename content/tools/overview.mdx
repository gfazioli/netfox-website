# Overview

The **Overview** is the landing tool — it's what you see the first time you open Netfox, and it's where you land at every launch. Think of it as the dashboard: in one screen you see *what's on your network*, *what looks risky*, *what your network's outward face is*, and *what's in the alert inbox*.

![Netfox Overview](/screenshot-overview.png)

## At a glance

Four cards across the top. Each is clickable and switches to the relevant tool. The two that track a proportion — Risk posture and Devices — show it as a **ring gauge**, the same reading as the [menu bar popover](/docs/menu-bar):

| Card | What it shows | Click target |
|---|---|---|
| **Risk posture** | A ring filling to the share of devices flagged high or medium, tinted by severity (green when all clear), with the breakdown underneath and the **Run Smart Scan** button | [Security tool](/docs/tools/security) |
| **Devices** | A ring filling to how many are online out of the total Netfox knows about | [Devices tool](/docs/tools/devices) |
| **Public IP** | Your network's outward IPv4 + a live chip when a VPN tunnel is active. Inline refresh button | [Devices tool](/docs/tools/devices) |
| **Alerts inbox** | Count of unread alerts since you last opened the bell | [Devices tool](/docs/tools/devices) |

Risk posture leads because it is the one card that asks you to do something. **Run Smart Scan** starts a scan across every untested device on your LAN, and works the same whether you have never scanned or want to re-check everything.

<Callout type="info">
Until v0.18.0 this number appeared twice — once in a banner across the top of the screen and again in the card below it, in two sizes a hundred points apart. The banner is gone and its button came down into the card.
</Callout>

<Callout type="info">
The **Public IP** card is opt-in. It's off by default; enable it from **Settings → Privacy → Show my public IP**. When on, Netfox queries a public-IP lookup service over HTTPS — that's the only outbound traffic the Overview generates.
</Callout>

## Network

Below the KPI cards sits a **Network** panel: the settings your network hands out to everything on it.

![The Network panel on the Overview](/screenshot-overview.png)

| Row | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| **Network name** | The Wi-Fi network this Mac is joined to |
| **IP address** | This Mac's address, with the size of the address range after the slash — `/24` means the first three numbers identify the network |
| **Router** | Where traffic leaves for the internet |
| **DNS** | The name servers the network tells its clients to use |
| **Domain** | The domain appended to bare names — it often names the internet provider or the router's maker |
| **DHCP server** | The device handing out addresses, and how long yours lasts |

These are the things you would otherwise go looking for in a router admin page, and they're the same for every device on the network — which is exactly why they live here once, rather than being repeated on all forty device panels.

<Callout type="info">
Netfox reads this from the lease **this Mac** holds. It never asks another device what its settings are, because there is no way to ask — so a device's own panel shows this under a clearly-labelled *Network context* group rather than presenting it as something measured on that device.
</Callout>

If this Mac is set to use name servers the network didn't nominate — a manual entry, a VPN, or a filtering service — an extra **This Mac uses** row appears, so you can see at a glance that your Mac and the rest of the network disagree.

## Link

Underneath the address is the physical layer: what the cable negotiated, what the radio is doing, and whether either of them is dropping anything. The **Network** panel above says how your connection is *configured*; this one says how it is *performing*.

On a wide window the two sit side by side. Narrow the window and they stack.

### Wired

Shown when an Ethernet interface is actually up — on a laptop with nothing plugged in, the section says so rather than disappearing.

| What you see | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| The negotiated speed | What the link settled on, in orange when it is below what the port can do |
| **Negotiated 1 Gb/s on a port that does 10 Gb/s** | The warning worth having. Nothing in macOS tells you this happened, and the cause is almost always the cable or the switch — Cat 5e runs 1 Gb/s, and 10 needs Cat 6a and a switch that speaks it |
| Full duplex · Flow control | Coloured, because there is a right answer |
| Auto | Plain, because auto-negotiation is how the link was configured rather than a grade — a hand-pinned link is unusual, not wrong |

### Wireless

| What you see | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| The generation and the rate | Wi-Fi 7 at 1.2 Gb/s, say |
| Band · width · channel | Only when the radio is associated. A radio that is switched off has no band, so those are left out rather than filled in with "Unknown" |
| **39 dB of headroom — signal −58 dBm against −97 dBm of noise** | How much room you have, not just how strong the signal is |
| **Steady at 1.2 Gb/s across two samples 2s apart** | The rate is what the link is doing now, not a property of it. Netfox takes two samples and says whether it held, rose or fell — during development it moved by half a gigabit in two seconds, on the same network, in the same room |

### Whether anything is being dropped

Two separate claims, because neither implies the other: what crossed the link **in the last two seconds** and whether any of it faulted, and the same question **since boot**. A link that has been clean for weeks can start dropping frames this second, and one that dropped a handful during a cable swap last month is clean now.

A link nothing crossed gets no clean bill of health. It says *idle* instead — it has not been shown to be fine, it has not been shown anything.

### How far away the delay starts

**First hop 2.9 ms, then 12 ms more past the router.** The first number is your own link; the second is everything beyond it. That split is the difference between a problem you can fix and one you can only wait out.

- A router that ignores pings is reported as untimeable rather than as a fault — plenty drop them by policy.
- Packet loss outranks speed. A flaky cable is quick right up until the packet that vanishes.
- Every figure is a **minimum**, never an average: one delayed reply drags a mean of five readings by a hundred milliseconds and moves the minimum by less than one.

<Callout type="info">
The first hop never leaves your network, so it is measured whether or not Netfox may reach the internet. The comparison past the router uses a public resolver and rides the same **Settings → Privacy** switch as the public-IP lookup — turn it off and the panel reports the first hop alone rather than inventing a comparison.
</Callout>

## Demo Mode

![Devices with Demo Mode active](/screenshot-demo-mode.png)

If you ever screenshot or screen-share the Overview, flip on **Demo Mode** with <Kbd>⌘</Kbd> <Kbd>⇧</Kbd> <Kbd>D</Kbd> first. Device names, MACs, IPv6, Wi-Fi SSIDs, and the public IP placeholder all collapse to generic labels — no real values leak into the recording. See [Settings → Privacy](/docs/settings#privacy) for the full mask reference.

## Roadmap teaser

A small footer card at the bottom of the Overview links to the public [Roadmap](/docs/roadmap), which lays out the modules still ahead. A module that has shipped leaves that list — it answers *what is not built yet*, not *what happened*.
