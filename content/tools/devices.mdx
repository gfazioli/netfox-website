# Devices

The **Devices** tool is the heart of Netfox — the live LAN device list, with a detail panel showing identity, history, ports, and recent activity for the currently selected row.

![Netfox Devices](/screenshot-devices.png)

## How devices are discovered

Five providers run in parallel and merge their results into one record per physical device:

| Provider | What it sees |
|---|---|
| **Bonjour / mDNS** | Apple devices (HomePods, Apple TVs), AirPlay receivers, AirPrint printers, anything advertising a `_*._tcp` service — including the model many of them declare about themselves |
| **System ARP cache** | Anything that has exchanged a packet on the LAN recently — phones, NAS, IoT plugs that don't advertise |
| **SSDP** | UPnP and DLNA devices — smart-TVs, media servers, some routers |
| **NetBIOS** | Windows shares and legacy NAS that advertise NetBIOS names |
| **Active ICMP** | Standard `ping` against IPs the system ARP cache holds but Bonjour didn't reach — catches devices that are alive but quiet |

<Callout type="info">
Discovery is **read-only by default**. The only outbound traffic the device list generates on its own is the ICMP echo from the active probe — no port scans, no DHCP poking, nothing intrusive. Port scanning only happens when you ask for it, from the [Security](/docs/tools/security) tool.
</Callout>

### Finding devices that never speak

Every provider above waits to be told. Bonjour and SSDP listen for whatever announces itself, the ARP cache holds whatever has already exchanged a packet with something, and the active probe only pings addresses one of the others has already produced. A device that is powered on, silent, and has never talked to this Mac is therefore invisible — not hard to find, but unreachable by construction.

**Find silent devices**, in the range control beside the search box, asks instead of listening. Give it a range of addresses and Netfox sends one echo request to each; whatever answers arrives through the ordinary pipeline, with its identity, its history and its alerts intact.

It doesn't invent rows. A reply proves a device is *there* and nothing else about it, so everything you then see on the row still comes from the providers above — a sweep makes a device discoverable, it doesn't describe it.

The range has to lie inside this Mac's own subnet, and Netfox **refuses** one that reaches outside rather than trimming it to fit: a clamped range sweeps a different set of addresses from the one you typed, and nothing on screen would say so. The menu offers your own subnet as a preset, named for the interface it belongs to, so the common case is one click.

<Callout type="info">
No password, no helper, no special privileges. A sweep is capped at 1,024 addresses per run; a typical home subnet is 254 of them, which takes roughly fifteen seconds. Netfox shows how many have answered while it runs, and you can stop it at any point.
</Callout>

## The device list

This Mac sits at the top, pinned, with its current Wi-Fi/Ethernet badge. Below: online devices first, then offline. Each row shows hostname / IP / vendor (or your custom tag) plus a dot — green online, gray offline.

### Search and filter
- The **search box**, pinned to the toolbar's trailing edge, filters by hostname, IPv4, IPv6, or MAC. Matches narrow the list live.
- The **status filter pill** scopes the list to *All / Online / Offline* without clearing the search. Your choice sticks — across switching tools and across restarts — so a list you've scoped to *Online* stays that way. The search box deliberately doesn't persist: a forgotten search term hides devices with nothing on screen to explain why.
- The **range control** scopes the list to a block of addresses. Set it to `192.168.1.1`–`192.168.1.2` and the list shows those two devices and nothing else: a range says which part of the network you care about, not only which part to ask. Sweeping sets it; **Filter only** sets it without the probe, for when you want the narrower list rather than a fresh look at it. While a range is active the control itself states it and offers to dismiss it, and the footer reads *2 in range — 33 on the network*. Naming that second number is the point, because a filter you have forgotten looks exactly like a network that has lost thirty devices. Like the search box, and unlike the status pill, it doesn't survive a restart.

![Searching the device list](/screenshot-device-search.png)

### Opening a device is something only you do

A scan finishing in the background used to be able to push a detail panel in front of you that you had never asked for. It cannot any more: the panel opens when you open it.

The other half of that rule is the one that matters once you are filtering. Ask for a specific device — from the search box, from an alert, from the History window — and Netfox shows it to you even when the current scope would hide it, relaxing whatever stands in the way rather than silently doing nothing. An explicit request outranks a filter you set earlier and have stopped thinking about.

### Bell badge
The bell in the toolbar carries a count of unread alerts. Click to open the inbox; click *Done* to mark them read and reset the badge to zero. The persistent **Alert History** (<Kbd>⌘</Kbd> <Kbd>⇧</Kbd> <Kbd>A</Kbd>) keeps every alert ever raised, even after you've cleared the inbox.

![Netfox alerts](/screenshot-alert-history.png)

## The detail panel

Selecting any device opens a panel of sections, each covering one thing you might want to know:

### Identity
MAC address, device kind (computer / phone / speaker / tablet / TV / printer / router / …), manufacturer, and the **Model** the device announces about itself. The **Services** row lists, in plain English, what the device offers on the network — AirPlay, file sharing, IPP printing, SSH, a web service, and so on — gathered from what each device advertises.

0.17.0 widened that list after measuring what a real network answers for, and one of the additions fixes a blind spot rather than adding a nicety: HomeKit accessories advertise the Accessory Protocol, which Netfox was not looking for, so they were arriving as **Unknown** while the label existed and was unreachable. Screen sharing, scanning (both the modern and older spellings that multifunction printers use), Matter and Spotify Connect are recognised too.

**Model** is the one identity signal on the network that isn't inferred. Where *Kind* and *Manufacturer* are worked out from the device's behaviour and its address, many devices state their model outright — `AppleTV5,3`, `Mac14,15`, `BRAVIA 4K GB ATV3` — and Netfox trusts that over everything else when deciding what a device is. It's what stops an Apple TV named "Living Room" from being filed as a phone: the name gives nothing away, and the services it speaks are the same ones a HomePod and a Mac use, but the model is unambiguous. Not everything publishes one; when nothing does, Netfox falls back to the services and the name as before.

**System** says which operating system a device runs, read from the same record it states its Model in — so Apple hardware only, and only when the device answers. A sleeping device publishes nothing, and most rows simply never grow one.

What the device actually states is an internal build number rather than the version you see in System Settings, so Netfox shows a name only where it can translate that number with certainty. Where it cannot, **the row is absent rather than showing a number that looks like a version and is not one**. That honesty has a cost worth knowing about: when a new macOS ships, its build number is one Netfox has not learned yet, so the row quietly disappears for Macs that have updated — which is why there is now a check that asks the Mac Netfox is running on whether it can name its own system, turning the next gap into something visible instead of a row that is silently not there.

**Manufacturer** comes from the registered block a MAC address belongs to, matched against the full public IEEE registry — around 53,000 assignments, so nearly every real device resolves. Where it doesn't, the reason is usually not a gap:

- **"Private address"** means the device invented its address rather than using the one burned into its hardware. Phones and laptops increasingly do this per network, for privacy. There is no manufacturer to look up — that's the complete answer, not a missing one.
- A blank Manufacturer means the address *is* a hardware address but its block isn't in the registry. That's rare, and mildly interesting in itself.

Netfox composes these into a one-line **plain-English identity** — *vendor · kind · services*, e.g. "Apple · Speaker · AirPlay". A device with no friendly name now **leads with that identity** in the list and the detail header (e.g. "Netgear · Router · Web service") instead of a bare IP address — the address drops to the subtitle, where it belongs. Devices you've named, or that report a hostname, keep that name up front as before.

In **Advanced Mode** (<Kbd>⌘</Kbd> <Kbd>⇧</Kbd> <Kbd>M</Kbd>) an extra *Engine ID* row appears — useful for cross-referencing logs.

### Service inspector
Turn on **Look up service info on open ports** (Settings → Privacy) and Netfox reads what a service *announces about itself* the moment it connects — the web server's response and page title on HTTP/HTTPS ports, the identification line an SSH server sends — and names the **product** behind it: a router's SSH shell, an HP printer, a NAS, a home-automation page. The recognised products appear together in a **Service inspector** section on the device's General tab, so a bare "port 8080 open" becomes "that's the printer's web admin".

It only reads what the service volunteers — no login attempt, nothing intrusive — and it's off until you opt in.

![The Service inspector naming the web product behind a printer's open port](/screenshot-device-inspector.png)

It reads SSH the same way — here it identifies the SSH service running on another device:

![The Service inspector reading a device's SSH service](/screenshot-device-inspector-ssh.png)

### Network
IPv4, IPv6, and connection type. The IPv4 row carries the size of the address range after it — `192.168.1.14` `/24` — which is a fact about the network the device sits on, not about the device. Copying the row still gives you the bare address. For this Mac, the connection type reflects the active interface (Wi-Fi / Ethernet / etc.). For other devices the value is blank — see the [FAQ](/docs/faq) for why we can't tell from the outside.

A device that does a job for the network says so here, as **Roles**:

- **Gateway** — traffic bound for the internet leaves through it
- **DNS resolver** — the network tells its clients to ask this device to look up names
- **DHCP server** — it hands out the addresses

On most home networks one box is all three. The interesting case is a resolver that *isn't* your router: that's an ad-blocker or a filtering service someone put in the path, and it's worth knowing you have one.

Under those, a collapsed **Network context** group holds the lease itself — subnet mask, router, name servers, domain, DHCP server. It's folded away because it is identical for every device on the network; expanding it here saves a trip to the [Overview](/docs/tools/overview#network) when you're already looking at a device.

![The Network context group expanded on a device](/screenshot-network-context.png)

### Link quality
How long a small packet takes to reach the device and come back, measured from your Mac.

![The Link quality panel](/screenshot-device-general.png)

| Row | What it tells you |
| --- | --- |
| **Latency** | The fastest round trip of the burst — the cleanest read on what the path costs when nothing is in the way |
| **Average** | The typical round trip |
| **Variability** | How much the individual trips differed. Erratic timing is what makes calls stutter and streams rebuffer, even when the average looks fine |
| **Packet loss** | How often the device didn't answer at all, across every check Netfox has stored — not just the last handful |

**Measure now** runs a fresh burst on demand. Otherwise the panel fills in on its own: Netfox already checks whether each device is reachable on a regular round, and now keeps the timings from those checks. The chart appears once there are enough measurements to be a trend rather than two dots.

<Callout type="warning">
This is **not** the device's Wi-Fi signal strength, and no app on your Mac can show you that. Signal is measured by whichever radio is receiving, so the only thing that knows how well your Apple TV hears your access point is the Apple TV. What Netfox measures is the path between your Mac and the device — which is the part you actually notice when something feels slow.
</Callout>

When the first reply of a burst takes far longer than the ones behind it, Netfox says so in plain words: that's a device letting its Wi-Fi radio sleep between frames and having to wake up to answer. It's normal for anything battery-powered, and without the explanation it reads as a problem. A mains-powered device on the same network shows no such gap.

Link quality is hidden for **This Mac** — the measurement would be a packet to your own address that never leaves the machine, so it would report a flawless link forever while saying nothing about the network.

### Activity
First seen, last seen, current status. Timestamps are relative (*2 minutes ago*) in normal mode and absolute ISO 8601 in Advanced Mode.

### Ports
Open services from the most recent security probe, grouped by category. Each port shows the service name, the port number, and a risk symbol if it triggered a finding. Ports you've added yourself appear under their own **Custom** section, labelled with the names you gave them — see [Security → Custom ports](/docs/tools/security#custom-ports). Empty section if the device has never been probed — use the **Probe Ports** button to run a scan, or run a network-wide scan from the [Security](/docs/tools/security) tool.

![The Ports tab, grouped by category](/screenshot-device-ports.png)

A picker above the list narrows it to one state, and each segment carries its count — **All**, **Open**, **Closed**, **Filtered**. The counts are worth reading on their own: *Filtered 3* on a device that answers everything else means something is dropping probes for exactly those three ports, which is a finding before you click anything.

**Closed and filtered are not the same thing**, and the difference is the useful part:

| | what happened | what you know |
|---|---|---|
| **Closed** | the device answered, refusing the connection | nothing is listening there — a definite answer |
| **Filtered** | no answer at all, the probe timed out | something is dropping the packets; whether a service is behind it is unknown |

Closed is a *no*. Filtered is a *silence*. A device that returns mostly closed ports is being straightforward with you; one that returns mostly filtered has a firewall in the way, and its real state is hidden.

#### Who is holding the port

On **This Mac**, each row also names what holds the port: the process, the user it runs as, and its process ID. Everything beyond that is in the sheet, one click away — three more sentences on every row would make this a control panel rather than a list.

The sheet says how the port is managed, whether closing it can be undone, and either the action or the reason there isn't one.

**Whether it comes back is read, not assumed**, and that distinction is the whole reason the line exists. A managed service set to stay running returns by itself after you stop it. One the system merely knows where to find does *not*: it stays stopped until something asks for it again. On the Mac this was measured on, 2 of 36 running services were set to restart themselves — so "it'll come back" would have been wrong about the other 34.

Where Netfox can't offer the action it says why rather than hiding the button, and the two refusals get different sentences. *Owned by another user* leaves you guessing; *a system service holds it, and below 1024 that needs an administrator* tells you that no amount of clicking will help.

### Recent activity
The last few transitions — online ↔ offline, IPv4 learned/changed, hostname changed, vendor learned, kind refined, ports opened/closed. *View Full History…* opens the complete timeline.

## Looking inside a service

The Ports tab tells you a port is *open*. Sometimes you want to know **what's actually answering** — is that web port a printer's admin page or a forgotten dev server? Is that database wide open or locked down? Tap any open port and Netfox opens a detail sheet that lets you look inside, read-only.

![The port detail sheet identifying the product behind an open web port](/screenshot-inspector-service-info.png)

The sheet has up to three tabs:

- **Overview** — the port, its category, current state, the recognised **product** (e.g. "HP printer · HP HTTP Server"), any risk finding, and an **Open with** row that hands off to the right system app: copy a database connection string, open a file share in Finder, an SSH session in Terminal. Netfox never logs in for you — it just opens the door.
- **Live** — for web ports, an in-app browser (below).
- **Raw** — the verbatim detail behind the summary, plus an on-demand protocol peek for non-web services (below).

The Raw tab is the unparsed truth — the exact response header a web server returned, an SSH server's identification line — for when you want the bytes, not Netfox's tidy summary:

![The Raw tab showing a web server's response header and status](/screenshot-inspector-raw.png)

### The in-app web viewer

For an open web port, the **Live** tab renders the device's own web interface — right inside Netfox, in a dedicated, resizable window.

![The Live tab's launcher for a device's web interface](/screenshot-inspector-web-launcher.png)

Click **Open Web Viewer** and the page loads in a viewer **locked to that one device**: it can't wander to anything else on your network or out to the internet, the page runs normally, and nothing is kept — no history, no cookies that outlive the window. It's the convenience of "just open the printer's page" without pointing your everyday browser at an untrusted LAN device.

![A printer's embedded web interface rendered inside Netfox's own web viewer](/screenshot-inspector-web-viewer.png)

#### Typing your own address

The address bar is editable, so you don't have to hunt for a link to get where you're going:

- **A path** — type `/admin` or `/web` and press <Kbd>Return</Kbd>.
- **Another port** — type `192.168.1.9:9000` to jump to a different service on the same device.
- **The other scheme** — switch between `http://` and `https://` the same way.

**The host stays locked.** It's the whole point of the viewer, so it isn't editable: type a different address and Netfox tells you it's locked to this device rather than following you there. Changing the port or scheme rebuilds the viewer's lock around the new one, so it's never loosened — just re-pointed, with your path preserved.

### Reading a non-web service

Web ports get a browser; everything else gets a **read-only peek**. Turn on **Inspect services on demand** (Settings → Privacy → Live inspection) and the Raw tab gains an **Inspect service** button.

![The Raw tab's Inspect service action](/screenshot-inspector-inspect-button.png)

It reads what the service announces on connect — an FTP or mail greeting, a database's handshake version. For the two databases that stay silent until spoken to (Redis, PostgreSQL) it sends a single harmless, non-authenticating hello and reads the reply. It never logs in, sends a credential, or issues a command.

From that, Netfox works out the service's **posture** — how safely it's exposed — and says it in plain English:

![A posture verdict: an open Redis reachable with no password](/screenshot-inspector-posture.png)

A database reachable **with no password** is the one you want to catch: right now anyone on your Wi-Fi could read or erase it. That verdict also feeds the [Security](/docs/tools/security) risk report, so an exposed dev database is flagged as soon as it's seen — not only after you go looking.

<Callout type="info">
Live inspection is **off by default** and strictly read-only — Netfox opens an outgoing connection only when you ask it to (or, with **Inspect automatically during scans**, as part of a scan you started), and never sends a password or a command. It's suppressed entirely in [Demo Mode](/docs/settings#privacy).
</Callout>

### Reading a TLS port

A port that speaks encrypted from the first byte — 443, 8443, a mail port like 993 or 995 — has to introduce itself before anything else happens. It presents a **certificate**, and it settles with your Mac on a **protocol version** and a **cipher suite**. The Overview tab gains a **Read TLS details** button for those ports, behind the same Live inspection switch, and one handshake answers all of it.

**The certificate** says who the server claims to be, who vouches for that claim, and until when:

- **Issued to** — the name the certificate is for. On a home device this is usually the device's own hostname.
- **Issued by** — who signed it. A public authority, or the device itself.
- **Expires** — the date it stops being valid.

Two of those combinations are worth knowing about, and Netfox names them rather than leaving you to compare dates and strings:

**It has expired.** Browsers and apps will refuse it or nag about it, and the fix is usually a renewal nobody remembers to do. A NAS or a camera quietly serving an expired certificate is a common find on a home network.

**It is signed by itself.** The certificate vouches for nothing except its own claim, because whoever issued it *is* the server. That is completely normal on a home device that generated its own on first boot, and it is worth a second look anywhere you did not expect it — a self-signed certificate is exactly what something impersonating a device on your network would also present.

Netfox also flags a certificate that is **not valid yet**, which almost always means the clock on the device is wrong rather than anything sinister, and one that **expires within three weeks**, so a renewal can happen before something breaks.

**The connection** says what the two ends actually agreed on:

- **Protocol** — the version of TLS in use, from the current 1.3 down to the versions that were retired years ago.
- **Cipher suite** — the exact set of algorithms protecting the session, under its standard name.

Two of those are worth naming as well:

**The version is obsolete.** TLS 1.0 and 1.1 were retired in 2021, so a server still speaking one of them has not been updated in a long time. Netfox deliberately accepts a version it would never use for its own traffic — refusing, which is what every browser now does, would report the oldest servers on your network as though they were not encrypted at all, and those are exactly the ones worth finding.

**The key exchange has no forward secrecy.** With the older static-RSA suites, traffic recorded today could be read later if the server's key ever leaks. Anything ECDHE, and everything in TLS 1.3, is safe from that.

One line under those two rows keeps them honest: what you see is what that server chose *for this connection*, not the full set it supports. A server that also accepts something older or weaker will not say so here — finding that out means offering it one thing at a time, over and over, which is a different and far noisier kind of tool.

<Callout type="info">
Netfox reads what it came for and then **deliberately hangs up without completing the connection**. It has what it came for by then, so there is no reason to finish: nothing further is exchanged and no session is established. Reading is not the same as trusting — an expired or self-signed certificate is precisely what this is for, so Netfox looks at what the server presents without judging whether to accept it.
</Callout>

TLS details are hidden in [Demo Mode](/docs/settings#privacy), for the same reason hostnames are: the name on a certificate is very often the name of the device.

### Reading a file-sharing port

Port 445 is file sharing — a NAS, a Windows PC, another Mac with sharing switched on. Which *version* of it a device speaks turns out to matter more than most protocol trivia, because one of them should not be running at all. The Overview tab gains a **Check the SMB version** button for that port, behind the same Live inspection switch.

Netfox asks two questions rather than one, and it has to:

- **Which version will you use?** The answer is the newest one your Mac and that device have in common — SMB 3.1.1 on anything current, something older on a device that has not been updated in a while.
- **Will you still accept SMB 1?** A separate question, asked separately, because the modern conversation cannot answer it. A device that speaks *only* SMB 1 does not reply to a modern request at all — so a single check would report it as nothing at all, which is the exact opposite of the truth.

Two answers get named:

**It still accepts SMB 1.** Microsoft retired that version and turned it off by default in 2017. It is the protocol the WannaCry outbreak spread over, and it is still quietly switched on in the corner of plenty of home networks — an old NAS, a printer with a scan-to-folder share, a router's USB disk. Turning it off is almost always a setting on the device itself. This can appear *alongside* a perfectly modern version on the line above, and that pairing is not a contradiction: it means the device offers both, and the old one is still there for anything that asks.

**This version cannot encrypt.** SMB 2.0.2 and 2.1 have no encryption to offer at all, so anything sitting between your Mac and that device can read what passes over it. Encryption arrives with SMB 3.

If Netfox cannot settle the SMB 1 question — the device stops answering, something on the path swallows the request — it says nothing at all rather than telling you it is off. A reassuring line about a question nobody managed to ask would be worse than no line.

<Callout type="info">
Both questions are the opening line of an ordinary file-sharing conversation, the part that happens before any login. Netfox sends no user name, no password and no share name, and it hangs up on the answer — nothing is mounted and nothing is listed.
</Callout>

The file-sharing card is hidden in [Demo Mode](/docs/settings#privacy). It never shows a name — but *this machine still answers SMB 1* is a fact about a real device on a real network, which is exactly what Demo Mode is there to keep off a screen.

## Local Services — what's listening on your own Mac

Select **This Mac** in the device list and you get a tab no other device has: **Local Services**.

![Local Services listing this Mac's listening ports, grouped by reachability](/screenshot-local-services.png)

There's a blind spot it exists to close. Netfox checks every device by connecting to it over the network, at its address on your Wi-Fi. That is the right way to check a printer, a NAS or a phone — and the wrong way to check the Mac you're sitting at. A development server on `127.0.0.1:3000` genuinely refuses connections from anywhere else, so the check reports it closed. Accurately, and uselessly: the most interesting services on the machine you care most about are precisely the ones a network check structurally cannot see.

So this list doesn't probe anything. It asks macOS directly which ports are listening, which means it sees all of them.

### The two groups

**Only on this Mac** — bound to a loopback address, so nothing else on your network can connect: not another computer, not your phone, not the router. Development servers, database engines and helper tools bind this way on purpose. These are the ones no network probe could ever have shown you.

**Reachable from the network** — not bound to loopback. Some listen on every network interface, others on this Mac's own address; either way, anything on your Wi-Fi can reach them. That's usually intended, since file sharing, AirPlay and remote access all work this way. It's worth a glance for one you don't recognise, because it's open to everything on the network, not only to you.

Each row is the port, the process holding it, and its process ID. An (i) beside each heading explains the group in plain English if you'd rather not keep the distinction in your head.

### Freeing a port

Netfox showed you that `node` is holding 3000, and until 0.17.0 the answer was still a terminal. Each row now has an action that asks that process to quit.

It asks first: the request is the polite kind, so a development server can save whatever it was holding before it goes. If it ignores that, Netfox offers to force it — and only then, because forcing loses unsaved work.

Where a single listener is held by several processes, all of them are asked, starting with the one that opened the socket. A web server that runs a worker per core is one entry in this list and several processes behind it; asking only the first would leave the others holding the port, and the port would look stuck for no visible reason.

<Callout type="info">
Some listeners can't be quit from here, and the row says which and why rather than failing when you press it. A service owned by another user or by the system needs privileges Netfox doesn't have and doesn't ask for. The action is also unavailable while Demo Mode is on, since Demo Mode replaces process names with placeholders — a confirmation naming something that isn't the real target would be worse than no button at all.
</Callout>

Netfox re-checks the port immediately before sending anything. A row is a snapshot of a moment that has already passed, and process IDs get reused, so what the list showed and what is holding the port right now are not guaranteed to be the same thing.

### Names instead of numbers

`3000` tells you nothing. `shop.local:3000` gives you somewhere to start.

Netfox reads your Mac's own hosts file and shows the names it finds — but where it shows them follows what they actually describe, because **a hosts entry points at an address, not at a port**.

The names on `127.0.0.1` and `::1` therefore belong to loopback itself, and reach every listener bound there — including every listener bound to *all* interfaces, since a wildcard bind answers on loopback too. That is how `shop.local:3000` reaches a server listening on `*`. Those names are stated **once, in the group heading**: *"On this Mac they also answer as localhost and shop.local."* Repeated on each row they would read as though each service owned its name, and on a machine with a single alias that puts the same name beside every unrelated thing you happen to be running.

A name on a **specific address** is the case that does tell rows apart, so it stays on the row: two listeners bound to two different addresses genuinely answer to two different names.

With several aliases the heading narrows things down; with exactly one it mostly tells you the file exists. Either way it points you at where to look rather than labelling what each service is.

<Callout type="info">
Nothing here needs a password, a permission prompt or a helper. Netfox reads what the system already knows about your own machine. Your own listeners come back complete; ones belonging to other user accounts or to the system may be partial, which is the cost of not asking for privileges. The whole tab is masked in [Demo Mode](/docs/settings#privacy) — a list of running processes is a software inventory, so process names, IDs and hosts-file names are all hidden before you share a screenshot.
</Callout>

## Tagging

Click **Edit Tag** on a device whose hostname is meaningless ("Espressif Inc.", "Computer-1") and assign:

- A custom name — replaces hostname everywhere in the UI
- A custom kind — overrides the auto-detected device kind
- A mute toggle — silences alerts for that device

Tags survive across launches and are masked when [Demo Mode](/docs/settings#privacy) is on. Wipe them from **Settings → Privacy → Clear custom tags** if you ever want a fresh slate.

## History timeline

The detail panel's **History** tab shows the most recent transitions inline, with a *View Full History…* link to the complete log:

![The per-device History tab](/screenshot-device-history.png)

Click *View Full History…* to open the per-device history sheet:

- Every observation Netfox has ever recorded for that device, newest first
- Categorised by kind (online/offline transition, hostname change, IP change, vendor change, port opened/closed, …)
- Filterable by event kind
- Exportable as JSON for sharing or diffing

### Port-state changes

When a probe — manual or scheduled — flips a port between **open** and not-open, the timeline records it: *"Port opened — 3000 (Node dev server)"* when a service appears, *"Port closed"* when it goes away. So the History captures how a device's **service exposure** changed over time, not just its presence: you can see that a dev server ran on port 3000 yesterday evening even though it's gone now. First-time observations don't record (learning a port's state isn't a change), and closed↔filtered flips are treated as reachability noise.

## Demo Mode

![Devices with Demo Mode active](/screenshot-demo-mode.png)

When [Demo Mode](/docs/settings#privacy) is on:
- Hostnames + custom tag names collapse to `Computer #1`, `Phone #2`, etc., numbered by device kind
- MAC addresses lose the last three octets (vendor OUI survives)
- IPv6 addresses lose everything past the first hextet
- IPv4 stays visible — RFC 1918 ranges don't leak anything network-specific
- Certificate details are hidden entirely — the name on a certificate is usually the device's own

The render-only masking is enough for safe screenshots; the underlying store is never touched.
